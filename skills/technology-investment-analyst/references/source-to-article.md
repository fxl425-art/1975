# From source text to analytical article

## 1. Build a source map

Extract into working notes:

- subject, time period, geography, and actors
- central claim and intended conclusion
- material facts, numbers, examples, and quotations
- causal claims and their unstated assumptions
- internal contradictions or changes in definition
- missing baseline, denominator, comparison, or time series
- source incentives and possible bias

Do not reproduce this map unless useful to the user.

## 2. Separate epistemic layers

Classify every important proposition as:

- **Source fact:** explicitly present in the supplied text
- **Source claim:** asserted or attributed in the text
- **Inference:** logically derived from the text
- **External knowledge:** not contained in the supplied text
- **Unknown:** necessary evidence is absent

In the article, use natural prose rather than tagging every sentence, but make attribution and uncertainty unmistakable. Do not introduce external knowledge as though it came from the supplied text.

## 3. Find the analytical spine

Choose one main tension, such as:

- growth versus value capture
- technological capability versus commercial adoption
- market enthusiasm versus embedded expectations
- product strength versus distribution weakness
- short-term cycle versus structural change
- large addressable market versus narrow obtainable market
- founder speed versus organizational scaling risk

State the article thesis in one sentence. Every section must advance, test, qualify, or apply that thesis.

## 4. Apply only useful lenses

Select two to four lenses based on the source:

- macro transmission and policy context
- market structure and value-chain profit pools
- customer problem, product wedge, and why now
- business model and unit economics
- distribution, retention, and scalability
- competitive response and moat durability
- capital needs, expectations, and valuation
- founders, learning velocity, and tenfold-scale constraints

Do not name-drop frameworks. Convert them into causal analysis.

## 5. Draft the article

Default structure:

1. **Title:** specific, concise, and thesis-led
2. **Standfirst:** two or three sentences stating why the issue matters and the main conclusion
3. **Opening:** introduce the central tension with the strongest fact or contrast
4. **Argument 1:** explain the underlying shift and mechanism
5. **Argument 2:** locate value capture, winners, constraints, or business consequences
6. **Argument 3:** test durability through product, competition, economics, or execution
7. **Counterargument:** steelman the strongest alternative interpretation
8. **Conclusion:** state implications, what to watch, and what would change the view

Use descriptive subheads for long articles. For short articles, prefer seamless prose.

## 6. Preserve fidelity

- Keep figures in their original units and periods unless conversion is shown.
- Retain meaningful qualifiers such as “may,” “adjusted,” or “management estimates.”
- Paraphrase by default; quote only language whose wording is analytically important.
- Do not fill source gaps with plausible-sounding detail.
- Point out when the source supports correlation but not causation.
- If the source is advocacy or marketing, analyze its incentives and omitted counterfactuals.

## 7. Edit for the requested style

Aim for lucid, compact, evidence-led prose with a strong point of view. Combine macro context, structured decomposition, investment relevance, and startup realism without copying any living writer, publication, consulting firm, bank, or accelerator's distinctive language.

Remove:

- chronological summary that lacks interpretation
- repetitive conclusions
- generic “opportunities and challenges” phrasing
- ornamental macro commentary
- unsupported predictions
- framework labels that interrupt the narrative

## 8. Final source audit

Check that:

- every material claim is supported by the supplied text, clearly inferred, or identified as external
- the article does not imply access to evidence that was not supplied
- the conclusion is stronger than a summary but no stronger than the evidence permits
- uncertainties and counterevidence remain visible
- the requested language, length, audience, and format are satisfied
